Vision Assist · Study Tier 1

Camera Geometry

Everything calibrate.py does, and why it has to. This is the layer all the multi-view work sits on — get it solid and COLMAP becomes comprehensible instead of magical.

01 What a camera actually destroys

The premise everything else follows from

A camera does not measure the world. It projects it — squashing three dimensions onto two. That is not a hardware limitation you can engineer around with a better sensor. It is what an image is.

One dimension is gone, and it is precisely the one you need: depth. Every difficulty in this project, from the plate volume to the ambiguity of a single ESP32 frame, traces back to this one fact. So the whole of Tier 1 is really one question — what exactly was lost, and what has to be supplied from outside to get it back?

02 The pinhole model

Similar triangles, and the equation they give you

Imagine a sealed box with a pinhole in one face. Light from a point in the world travels in a straight line through that hole and lands on the back wall. One point outside, one point inside, one straight line connecting them.

That is the entire model. Real lenses gather far more light than a pinhole can, and we will pay for that convenience in §07 — but geometrically, a well-made camera behaves like this box.

Place the camera centre at the origin, pointing down the Z axis. A point in the world sits at (X, Y, Z). The image forms on a plane at distance f from the centre — the focal length.

The shaded triangle from O to the image, and the large triangle from O to the object, are similar — same angles, different size. So their sides are in the same ratio:

x / f = X / Z
x = f · X / Z     y = f · Y / Z the perspective projection equation

That Z in the denominator is perspective itself. Double the distance, halve the image. It is why railway tracks converge and why the far end of your dinner plate looks narrower than the near end.

03 Scale ambiguity, derived

The most important consequence in this entire project

Look hard at x = f · X / Z. The world quantities X and Z never appear alone — only as the ratio X/Z.

So take any object and replace it with one twice as large, twice as far away. X doubles, Z doubles, the ratio is unchanged, and x is identical. The two scenes produce pixel-for-pixel the same image.

Keystone

A single image cannot tell you the size of anything. Not with a better model, not with more training data, not with a larger network. The information is not in the image.

Metric scale must be injected from outside: a marker of known size, a known camera baseline, or a learned prior about how big things usually are. This is why your ArUco sheet exists, and why make_aruco.py nags you to measure the printed marker.

Two consequences worth carrying forward. First, structure-from-motion has this problem too — many views pin down shape beautifully but leave overall scale free, which is why COLMAP alone will not give you millimetres. Second, because volume is a cubed quantity, a 5% error in your scale factor becomes roughly a 16% error in grams. Your scale reference is the highest-leverage number in the project.

04 From millimetres to pixels

The intrinsic matrix K, one element at a time

The equation in §02 gives x in physical units on a sensor. But you never see millimetres — you see pixel indices. Two adjustments close that gap.

Focal length in pixels

Divide the physical focal length by the physical width of one pixel. If f is 4 mm and pixels are 1.4 µm wide, then fx ≈ 2857 pixels. You get two numbers, fx and fy, because pixels need not be perfectly square — though on any modern sensor they essentially are, which gives you a free sanity check.

Moving the origin

The projection equation puts the origin on the optical axis, at the image centre. But array indexing puts (0, 0) at the top-left corner. So you add an offset — the principal point (cx, cy), roughly at the image centre.

u = fx · X/Z + cx      v = fy · Y/Z + cy projection, in pixels

Package the four numbers into a matrix and you have the intrinsic matrix:

    ⎡ fx   0   cx ⎤
K = ⎢  0   fy   cy ⎥
    ⎣  0    0    1 ⎦ four numbers that fully describe the camera's optics

K depends only on the camera and its resolution — not on where the camera is or what it is looking at. Calibrate once, reuse forever. That is why it goes in a file.

In your code

calibrate.py writes exactly these four numbers to intrinsics.json, alongside the resolution they were measured at. The resolution matters: fx in pixels scales with image width, so intrinsics measured at 1280×720 are wrong at 1920×1080. This is why the capture step pins the resolution and the solve step refuses to mix image sizes.

05 Homogeneous coordinates

Why there is a 1 in the corner of that matrix

Projection involves dividing by Z, and division is not a linear operation — you cannot write it as a matrix multiply. That is inconvenient, because linear algebra is where all the good tools live.

The fix is a bookkeeping trick. Represent a 2D point (u, v) by the 3-vector (u, v, 1), and declare that scaling the whole vector changes nothing: (u, v, 1) and (2u, 2v, 2) are the same point. To recover ordinary coordinates, divide by the last element.

s · [u, v, 1]ᵀ = K · [X, Y, Z]ᵀ the division is deferred, not avoided

Now the matrix multiply is linear, and the awkward division happens once at the end when you normalise by s — which turns out to equal Z. Nothing deep is going on; it is a change of notation that lets you compose projections, rotations, and translations as a single chain of matrices.

It also explains that scale ambiguity again, from a different angle: s is free. A point and the same point scaled are indistinguishable, in the algebra exactly as in the picture.

06 Extrinsics

Where the camera is standing

K describes the camera's optics. It says nothing about where the camera is. For that you need a rotation R (3×3, which way it points) and a translation t (3×1, where it sits) that carry world coordinates into camera coordinates.

s · [u, v, 1]ᵀ = K · [ R | t ] · [X, Y, Z, 1]ᵀ the full projection equation — memorise this one

Read it right to left: a world point, moved into the camera's frame by [R|t], then projected to pixels by K.

The split matters enormously. K is one set of numbers for the whole camera. [R|t] is a different set for every single photograph. Twenty calibration images means one K and twenty [R|t] pairs.

Hold onto that, because it is the shape of structure-from-motion too. COLMAP solves for one K per camera and one pose per image — the same decomposition, at larger scale.

In your code

cv2.calibrateCamera returns rvecs and tvecs — lists with one entry per input image, each (3,1). The rotation comes back as a 3-element Rodrigues vector (axis × angle) rather than a 3×3 matrix, because a rotation genuinely has only 3 degrees of freedom and optimisers prefer the compact form. cv2.Rodrigues() converts between them.

07 Lens distortion

Where real optics stop obeying the model

A pinhole is geometrically perfect and optically useless — it passes almost no light. Real cameras use lenses, and lenses bend rays slightly wrong, especially far from the centre. The symptom is that straight lines in the world come out curved in the image.

OpenCV models this with five coefficients. Three are radial (k1, k2, k3), describing displacement outward or inward along the radius. Two are tangential (p1, p2), covering the case where the lens is not perfectly parallel to the sensor.

x' = x (1 + k₁r² + k₂r⁴ + k₃r⁶) + tangential terms r = distance from the principal point

Notice that every term carries a power of r, so at the centre (r = 0) distortion vanishes entirely. That matches the picture: the middle of your image is trustworthy, the corners are not.

Sign tells you the type. Negative k1 is barrel distortion — lines bow outward, typical of wide-angle lenses and of essentially every webcam and phone camera you will use. Positive is pincushion.

Why you cannot skip this

Uncorrected distortion silently bends your measurements. A plate rim near the frame edge is reported at the wrong radius, and that error propagates straight into your scale factor and then, cubed, into grams. It is also the reason to keep the marker and the plate away from the extreme corners when you capture.

08 How calibration actually works

Counting equations against unknowns

You now have a model with unknowns in it. Calibration is the process of pinning them down, and it works by a simple trick: photograph something whose 3D geometry you already know.

A chessboard is ideal. Its corners are perfectly regular, so if you measure one square you know every corner's position. And it is flat, so you can declare the board's own plane to be Z = 0 and write every corner as (i·s, j·s, 0) for square size s.

Now count. A 9×6 board has 54 corners. Each corner, in each view, gives you two equations — one for u, one for v. With 20 views:

QuantityCountWhere it comes from
Equations2,16054 corners × 20 views × 2
Intrinsic unknowns4fx, fy, cx, cy
Distortion unknowns5k1, k2, p1, p2, k3
Pose unknowns1206 per view × 20 views
Total unknowns129

2,160 equations for 129 unknowns. Massively overdetermined — which is exactly what you want, because every measurement carries noise. No solution satisfies all 2,160 equations exactly, so you look for the one that comes closest, in the least-squares sense. The redundancy is what averages the noise away.

Why pose variety matters more than image count

Here is the trap. Twenty photographs of the board held flat-on, parallel to the sensor, at various distances, are nearly useless — and it is §03 that explains why. A flat board twice as far and twice as large projects identically. Held parallel, the board gives you no way to separate focal length from distance; the two trade off against each other and the solution is unstable.

Tilt the board and the near edge projects larger than the far edge. That foreshortening is depth information, and it forces the solver to commit to a particular focal length. Ten well-varied views beat fifty lazy ones.

09 Reprojection error

The number that governs everything downstream

Once you have candidate values for K, dist, and each [R|t], you can run the model forwards: take a corner whose 3D position you know, push it through the full projection equation, and predict where it should land in the image.

You also know where it actually landed, because the corner detector found it. The distance between prediction and observation, in pixels, is the reprojection error.

Calibration is nothing more than: choose the parameters that make the total squared residual as small as possible. The problem is non-linear because of the division by Z and the distortion polynomial, so OpenCV solves it iteratively with Levenberg–Marquardt, starting from a closed-form initial guess.

The bridge to Tier 2

Reprojection error is exactly what bundle adjustment minimises inside COLMAP. The difference is only scale: calibration optimises one camera over a known board, while bundle adjustment optimises many cameras and millions of unknown 3D points at once. Same objective function, same intuition. You are meeting the central idea of multi-view geometry in its simplest possible form.

Reading the number

RMSVerdictWhat to do
< 0.5 pxExcellentProceed
0.5 – 1.0 pxFine hereProceed
> 1.0 pxSomething is wrongCheck focus, motion blur, and the square size you typed

A suspiciously low error deserves scrutiny too. Under about 0.1 px from a real camera usually means too few views, or all views too similar — the model has enough freedom to fit your handful of images without being genuinely constrained.

10 The code, line by line

calibrate.py, with real shapes and dtypes

Building the object points

objp = np.zeros((cols * rows, 3), np.float32)
objp[:, :2] = np.mgrid[0:cols, 0:rows].T.reshape(-1, 2)
objp *= args.squares

This constructs the board's corners in the board's own coordinate frame. Working through it for a 9×6 board:

ExpressionShapeMeaning
np.mgrid[0:9, 0:6](2, 9, 6)two index grids, x and y
.T(6, 9, 2)transposed so pairs sit last
.reshape(-1, 2)(54, 2)flat list of (x, y) index pairs
objp(54, 3)float32, third column all zeros

The first few rows come out [0,0,0], [25,0,0], [50,0,0] — x advancing fastest, which matches the order findChessboardCorners returns its corners in. That correspondence is not decorative; if the two orderings disagreed, you would be pairing corner 1 with corner 47 and the solve would produce nonsense.

The third column stays zero because the board is planar. float32 is required — OpenCV will reject float64 here.

Finding the corners

found, corners = cv2.findChessboardCorners(
    gray, board,
    cv2.CALIB_CB_ADAPTIVE_THRESH + cv2.CALIB_CB_NORMALIZE_IMAGE,
)
ItemValue
input graysingle-channel uint8 image
input board(cols, rows) of inner corners
returns foundbool — all corners located, or none
returns corners(54, 1, 2) float32

That (54, 1, 2) shape with its vestigial middle axis is an OpenCV convention you will meet constantly. It is not a mistake.

ADAPTIVE_THRESH thresholds locally rather than globally, which is what lets the detector cope with uneven lighting across the board. NORMALIZE_IMAGE equalises contrast first.

The function is all-or-nothing: it returns every corner or none at all. And remember the convention — inner corners, not squares. A board printed with 10×7 squares is 9x6 here.

Refining to sub-pixel

corners = cv2.cornerSubPix(gray, corners, (11, 11), (-1, -1), criteria)

The detector returns corners on the integer pixel grid. That is not accurate enough — you are trying to measure a camera to a fraction of a pixel. cornerSubPix examines the intensity gradients in an 11×11 window around each corner and slides it to the point where the gradients are most consistent with a true corner, typically improving precision several-fold.

(-1, -1) means "no dead zone in the middle of the window". The criteria tuple stops the iteration at 30 rounds or when the corner moves less than 0.001 px, whichever comes first.

The solve

rms, K, dist, rvecs, tvecs = cv2.calibrateCamera(
    obj_points, img_points, image_size, None, None
)
ReturnedType / shapeMeaning
rmsfloatRMS reprojection error, pixels
K(3, 3)the intrinsic matrix
dist(1, 5)k1, k2, p1, p2, k3
rvecslist of N × (3, 1)rotation per view, Rodrigues form
tvecslist of N × (3, 1)translation per view

Note image_size is (width, height), while NumPy's .shape gives (height, width). Hence the [::-1] in the code. Getting this backwards is a classic silent bug — it will not crash, it will just give you a subtly wrong camera.

The two None arguments are optional initial guesses for K and dist. Passing None asks OpenCV to estimate them from scratch.

Per-view error

proj, _ = cv2.projectPoints(obj_points[i], rvecs[i], tvecs[i], K, dist)
err = cv2.norm(img_points[i], proj, cv2.NORM_L2) / len(proj)

This is §09 made executable — the model run forwards, then compared against observation. projectPoints returns (54, 1, 2), matching the detector's shape so the two can be differenced directly.

The aggregate rms hides bad individual views. This loop exposes them, and it is genuinely useful: one blurred image can drag the whole solve. Delete the worst offenders and re-run.

11 Running it

Process, inputs, outputs, and what to check

Before you touch the keyboard

  1. Print the chessboard. Tape it flat to stiff card — a curled board is a curved object, and the whole method assumes it is planar. This is the single most common cause of a bad calibration.
  2. Measure one square with a steel rule. Do not trust the size the PDF claims; printers rescale silently.
  3. Write the measured value into NOTES.md.

Capture

python src/calibrate.py capture --out captures/laptop

A live window opens. Corners are drawn in colour when the board is found; SPACE saves a frame, Q quits. Collect 20–30 views and vary them deliberately: near and far, tilted left, right, up, down, and with the board in each corner of the frame as well as the centre. Edge coverage is what constrains the distortion coefficients, since distortion is invisible at the centre.

Hold still for each grab. Motion blur moves corners.

Solve

python src/calibrate.py solve --images captures/laptop --squares 25.0 --preview

Substitute your measured square size in millimetres. Everything downstream inherits its units from this one number.

Reading the output

Four checks, in order:

  1. RMS under 1.0 px. If not, look at the worst-view list the script prints and delete the offenders.
  2. cx, cy near the image centre. For 1280×720 expect roughly (640, 360). Far off means bad input, not an exotic camera.
  3. fx/fy ≈ 1.000. Pixels are square on any sensor you will use. A ratio of 1.05 means something is wrong.
  4. The undistortion preview. Lines that bowed outward should be straight. This is your eyes confirming what the numbers claim.

The result lands in intrinsics.json. Then do it again for your phone, at a locked resolution — that is the camera you will orbit plates with.

Log it

Record the RMS, the four intrinsics, and the view count in NOTES.md for both cameras. In week 9 you will need these numbers for the paper's setup section, and reconstructing them later from memory is not an option.

Tier 1 exit criteria

  2. What do fx and cx physically represent, and why does fx change if you switch resolution?
  3. Why must the chessboard be flat, and why must it be photographed at varied angles?
  4. Define reprojection error. Why is it the right thing to minimise rather than, say, 3D distance?
  5. Your calibration reports RMS 3.2 px. Name three plausible causes.
  6. Where does lens distortion vanish, and why does that make edge coverage important during capture?
  7. A 5% error in your scale factor produces roughly what error in estimated grams?

If any of these are shaky, re-read the relevant section before starting Tier 2. Multi-view geometry assumes all of it, and debugging COLMAP without it is guesswork.

Question 4 has a subtlety worth chasing: we minimise error in the image, where the measurement noise actually lives, rather than in 3D where it would be warped by the projection. That reasoning carries directly into bundle adjustment.
