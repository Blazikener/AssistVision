\begin{abstract}
Many older adults eat less than they are served. In hospital, eating little
predicts death even after adjusting for illness, yet intake is usually judged by
eye. AssistVision is a clip-on camera for ordinary spectacles (Seeed XIAO
ESP32S3 Sense, a small LiPo and two 3D-printed pods, about INR~2,000--2,900 in
parts) worn by a carer. One button press records a 13-frame burst while the
carer looks across the plate, once when the meal is served and once when it is
cleared; the host reconstructs both scenes and reports the share of the meal
eaten. A companion app keeps the meal log, alerts family after a run of
low-intake meals and prints a weekly report for the doctor. To choose the
vision pipeline we test \input{tables/n_models.tex}monocular depth models on
507 Nutrition5k dishes and dense multi-view stereo on
\input{tables/n_synth.tex}synthetic plates with exact volume, plus 12
before/after meal pairs seen from a turntable path and from a simulated head
sweep.

A share of the whole plate cannot say that the rice went and the dal stayed, so
the app also splits each plate into its dishes from the two photos and turns
what was left into protein eaten per day; after three days under 1.0\,g per kg

\section{Introduction}
Older adults who eat too little lose weight and muscle and recover more slowly
from illness. In the NutritionDay survey of 16,290 hospitalised adults, more
than half did not finish the meal they were given, and eating only about a
quarter of it went with an adjusted hazard ratio of 2.10 for dying in hospital
over the next 30 days (3.02 for eating nothing)~\cite{hiesmayr2009nutritionday}. The
ESPEN guideline for geriatrics recommends regular malnutrition screening for
every older person~\cite{volkert2019espen}. Screening and follow-up need a
number for how much was eaten. On wards that number usually comes from a
nurse's visual estimate of the leftovers; checked against weighing, such
estimates hold up on average across a group but are much less reliable for a
single patient~\cite{heighington2022visual,kawasaki2016criterion}. At home
there is often no record at all, and children living in another city learn of a
decline only once it shows as weight loss.

Weighing the plate before and after the meal is the reference method in those
validation studies~\cite{kawasaki2016criterion}. It is accurate but tedious,
so it is rarely done as routine, and a scale at every seat costs money and
space. We take the measurement to the person who is already at the table: a
carer wearing AssistVision's clip-on camera glasses presses a button at
serving and at clearing, the host compares the two scenes, and the companion
app shows the share eaten to family members and the doctor.

One photo cannot tell a small plate held close from a big plate further away;
both land on the same pixels. The scale has to be brought in from somewhere
else. A printed marker can supply it, and so can a depth sensor, a camera that
moves along a known path, or a network that has learned typical sizes. Each
costs something different, and we measured how much. For the glasses we also
asked a narrower question: is a four-second head turn steady enough that the
served and cleared volumes can be divided by each other?

This paper makes six contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A meal-intake monitor for older adults (Sec.~\ref{sec:care}). One
wearable camera takes a burst when the plate arrives and another when it goes
back. The share eaten comes from the pair, a pair that cannot be right (more
food after than before) is flagged, and a small app shows the family and the
doctor the meals, a two-week trend, a low-intake alert and a weekly report.
\item A per-dish reading of the same two photos (E7). It finds which dish came
back and how much protein went back with it. Its protein alert separates two
cases a doctor treats differently: the person leaves the dal, or the menu
never had enough protein to begin with.
\item Camera glasses anyone can print and build (Sec.~\ref{sec:hw}). The pods
are parametric, so they fit a cheap frame. We release the burst firmware, the
laptop client and a build guide with a picture for every step.
\item Monocular depth models tested on food with real depth (Nutrition5k). We
kept the calibration a phone could really do apart from the oracle kind that
peeks at the ground truth.
\item Rendered plates with exact mesh volumes. Against them we tested dense MVS
and changed what a builder controls: number of views, resolution, JPEG quality,
marker error and plate material. Partly eaten plates were then seen along a
turntable path (E5) and along a simulated head turn (E6).
\item The design choices we made and the ones we dropped
(Sec.~\ref{sec:design}), each with the measurement or assumption behind it.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Meal intake in care settings.} In hospitals the reference for intake is
weighed plate waste. On the ward, staff usually look at the tray and mark a
quarter, a half or a percentage. A review that pooled 14 validation studies of
this visual method found it held up for groups of patients but much less for a
single patient, and only two of the studies used any modern
technology~\cite{heighington2022visual}. When nursing assistants' estimates in a
community hospital were checked against weighed trays, the gap was significant,
with Bland--Altman limits from $-121$ to $+147$\,kcal per
tray~\cite{kawasaki2016criterion}. The glasses are meant to replace the
look-and-mark step. Weighing stays the reference.
\textbf{Food volume.} Nutrition5k~\cite{thames2021nutrition5k} provides
overhead RGB-D video and weighed dishes; its depth-based volume baseline uses a
constant per-pixel footprint at the capture-plane distance. Multi-view food
reconstruction has used structure-from-motion with reference cards and dense
stereo~\cite{dehais2017two,lo2020image}. \textbf{Monocular depth.} Relative
depth models (MiDaS~\cite{ranftl2020midas}, Depth Anything~\cite{yang2024depthanything,yang2024depthanythingv2})
predict depth up to an unknown affine transform of inverse depth; metric models
(ZoeDepth~\cite{bhat2023zoedepth}, Depth Pro~\cite{bochkovskii2024depthpro},
metric-finetuned Depth Anything V2) predict absolute depth from priors learned on
room- and street-scale scenes. \textbf{Reconstruction.} We use COLMAP~\cite{schoenberger2016sfm}
for SfM and OpenMVS for dense MVS.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Shared volume integral}
Every method ends with points $P$ in metres and a plane $(\mathbf n, d)$ for
the table, its normal pointing at the camera. For each point we take the height
above that plane, $h=\mathbf n^\top\mathbf p+d$, and its position on the plane
along two axes at right angles. We drop the points into square cells of side
$c$ and keep the median height in each cell. A hole inside the food outline
takes the value of the nearest filled cell, heights below the plane become
zero, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vol}
V=c^2\sum_{\text{cells}}\max(h,0).
\end{equation}
Monocular depth, multi-view and the depth sensor all go through this one sum.
When their volumes differ, it is the geometry that differs.

\subsection{Metric scale from the marker mat}\label{sec:scale}
We find the marker corners in each view and triangulate them with the SfM
cameras. The scale $s$ is the printed side length over the median triangulated
one. Volume goes with the cube of length, so if $s$ is off by $\epsilon$ the
volume is off by $(1+\epsilon)^3-1\approx3\epsilon$. A 1\,\% slip in scale
costs about 3\,\% of volume, which Sec.~\ref{sec:synth} checks.

\subsection{Monocular protocols}\label{sec:anchor}
\textbf{Raw}: the model's metric output is used as-is. \textbf{Anchor}: the
prediction is calibrated using only the support plane (the table in
Nutrition5k, the marker squares beside our plate), never the food; metric outputs get one
scale, relative outputs an affine map of inverse depth. \textbf{Oracle}: the
same maps fitted to ground-truth depth over the whole dish. That is the best
the model could do, and a real device never has that depth. For relative
models we log the condition number of the anchor fit: when the table faces the
camera head-on, its inverse depth is almost the same everywhere, and scale and
shift cannot be told apart.
